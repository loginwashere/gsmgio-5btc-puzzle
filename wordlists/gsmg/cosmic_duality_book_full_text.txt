## p.6-7 — Essay opening: "The Unity of Opposites"

Sidebar photo caption: "Nineteen feet wide and twenty-three feet high, this
seventh-century carving honors the Hindu god Shiva, a multifaced deity in whom all
opposites combine."

Opening text: "'In the beginning, God created the heaven and the earth,' recounts the
biblical Book of Genesis. 'And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was
upon the face of the deep.... And God said, Let there be light: And there was light....
And God divided the light from the darkness.... And the evening and the morning were
the first day.' Creation seems to imply contrast: Light reveals darkness; morning ends
the night. As the Genesis story unfolds, other universal opposites emerge: male and
female, good and evil, and finally life and death. These and other paired energies
dominate creation tales around the world.

For most societies, however, the great dualities of nature are not unalterably opposed.
Instead, such forces combine and intertwine. In the sacred artifacts pictured here and
on the following pages, light confronts darkness, the genders commingle, and death
dances with life in scenes of rebirth and resurrection. Even good and evil are conjoined
in such ambiguous figures as the snake — to some cultures a magical symbol of health and
good fortune, to others a harbinger of malice and deceit."

## p.12-13 — "Life and Death in an Endless Cycle" (sidebar)

Caption (scarab amulet): Found in the tomb of Tutankhamen, the lapis amulet at left
depicts a scarab, or dung beetle. Scarabs were symbols of eternal life to ancient
Egyptians, who believed the insects came to life spontaneously in pellets of dung.

Caption (Celtic carving): Carved by an anonymous Celt around the second century BC,
the warrior at right looks back to heroic feats and forward to his inescapable death.
The Celts believed a fatalistic acceptance of death inspired greater courage in battle.

Caption (phoenix bestiary): In a supposed case of natural resurrection, the legendary
phoenix -- pictured at right in a sixteenth-century German bestiary -- was said to live
500 years, immolate itself on a pyre, then reappear in the ashes of its burned corpse.

Body text: "Life follows upon death. Death is the beginning of life. Who knows when the
end is reached?" wrote the fourth-century-BC Taoist Chuang Chou, who like most Eastern
philosophers assumed human beings were reincarnated after death. "If then life and
death are but consecutive states, what need have I to complain?"
As depicted in ageless images like the Celtic stone carving above, the human need to
reconcile existing life with inevitable death -- one's own, or of loved ones, even the
autumnal death of vegetation and the waning of the winter sun -- finds expression in
every culture. Some religious faiths view death as final, with human identity surviving
only in the memories of those left behind. But in other belief systems -- from that of
ancient Egypt to the Taoism of Chuang Chou, to modern Buddhism, Christianity, Islam, and
the Hindu beliefs represented at right -- death is not the end but the beginning of new
life. Symbolizing the concept of rebirth through sacred images and fabulous tales, these
traditions portray death as a doorway into another existence: in some faiths a paradise
of eternal bliss or a hell of suffering, in others simply an opportunity to live another
life on this earth.

Caption (Kali painting): The Hindu goddess Kali stands over her consort Shiva's awakened
persona while his double lies sleeping in this eighteenth-century painting. Worshiped as
the source of both life and death, Kali holds symbols of vitality -- the lotus of
regeneration and the bowl of abundance -- in her left hands, while her right hands wield
the sword and scissors of physical mortality. The goddess wears a necklace of severed
human heads.

## p.14-15 

Ambivalent Symbols of Vice and Virtue

Just as any human being combines traits of both good and evil, so the recurrent symbols 
of human culture have often taken on a mixed significance, venerated in one era only to 
be despised in another. In ancient Greece, for example, most homes displayed paintings of 
beneficent snakes over the family altar. The snakes were considered symbols of earth magic, 
fertility, and regeneration. But in societies fearful of its sometimes deadly bite, 
the serpent became a guileful messenger of evil, typified by the Satanic specimen 
that tempted Eve in the Garden of Eden and brought about humankind's fall from grace.

Similarly, the unicorn with its thrusting horn seemed the embodiment of insatiable lust 
to many medieval European scholars. A twelfth-century rule book for nuns described the 
beast as wicked, wrathful, and an emblem for violent people, and a unicorn's horn soon 
came to be regarded as highly aphrodisiac. Yet the very pursuit of unicorn horns to make 
the treasured love potion led others to view the mythical beast as an innocent victim. 
In the traditions that shaped modern beliefs about the unicorn, trusting passivity came 
to the fore as the unicorn became the archetype of purity, virtue, and innocence betrayed.

Left caption

A serpentine Hindu Naga guards the source of wisdom and sacred knowledge in the 
seventh-century Indian carving at upper left. Benevolent to the virtuous but capable 
of terrible violence against blasphemers, such serpent demigods were said to carry 
within themselves the forces of darkness and light, earth and water. 
To the ancient Egyptians, however, the serpent had no benevolent side. 
Every night, the snake demon Apophis, spirit of darkness, attacked the sun god Ra, 
seen here as a cat. As depicted in the fourteenth-century BC painting from 
the Book of the Dead at left, Ra solved that problem by cutting off the demon's head.

Right caption

In the sixteenth-century allegory above, a diminutive unicorn representing chastity 
peacefully rests its head on a maiden's lap. For much of recorded history, however, 
unicorns were more commonly viewed as muscular, lustful beasts with piercing horns. 
That perception of the creature holds full sway in Albrecht Dürer's 1516 engraving 
The Abduction of Proserpina (left), in which a unicorn, nostrils flared, 
hoofs pawing the earth, seems a willing accomplice to a forcible kidnapping.

## p.22-25 — The Pelasgian and other creation myths

The Pelasgian creation myth: before all things there was Chaos. The goddess Eurynome
("naked... the first to arise from Chaos") separated sea from sky to have somewhere to
dance, which stirred up the north wind Boreas behind her; she transformed the wind into
a great serpent named Ophion. Ophion coupled with Eurynome; she took the form of a dove
and laid the "universal egg," which the serpent coiled around until it hatched, releasing
the sun, moon, and all heavenly bodies. Eurynome and Ophion then lived on Mount Olympus
until Ophion claimed he alone had created the universe; enraged, Eurynome kicked out his
teeth and banished him to the underworld. From Ophion's teeth Eurynome created the first
man, Pelasgus, and originated the Pelasgian race. Etymological aside: the German word for
"sin," Sünde, is related to the word for "sever"/"separate."

Hesiod's "Theogony" (Greek, 8th century BC): Gaia (earth) rose from Chaos, bore Uranus
(heaven/sky). Gaia and Uranus coupled and produced the Titans (six female, six male) and
the monstrous Cyclopes, whom Uranus imprisoned beneath the earth. Enraged, Gaia conspired
with her last-born Titan son, Cronos, who castrated his father Uranus with a sickle; the
Furies arose from Uranus's wound. Among the Titans' conflicts, Zeus was born and came to
rule the universe. Cronos was later eviscerated by his own son Zeus. A Babylonian myth:
heaven and earth fashioned from the body of the dragon Tiamat, cut in two by the warrior
Marduk. Maori myth: Tane-mahuta, son of sky father Pangi and earth mother Papa, rends his
parents apart to be born. Norse myth: the hermaphroditic giant Ymir slain by his three
grandsons — "Of Ymir's flesh the earth was fashioned, / And of his sweat the sea; /
Crags of his bones, trees of his hair, / And of his skull the sky."

Hindu Upanishads: the Supreme Self (sometimes called Brahma) was alone, took no delight
in solitary existence, so "he made himself fall into two," creating a man and a woman —
the origin of the human race, who then transformed themselves into pairs of animals (cow
and bull, mare and stallion, etc.) all the way down to the ants. "Brahma came to be
regarded as a sort of all-in-one duality."

Sidebar image: "PORTAE LVCIS" (Gate of Light), a 1516 illustration of a Jewish cabalist
holding the Tree of Life, ten spheres/facets of God including "the opposing male on the
right and female on the left and a harmonizing midline" (Sefirot: Keter, Chochmah, Binah,
Chesed/Gedulah, Gevurah, Tiferet, Netzach, Hod, Yesod, Malchut — labeled in the image as
כתר, חכמה, בינה, גבורה, גדולה, תפארת, נצח, הוד, יסוד, מלכות).

Two "God and the Devil" creation legends: (1) A Bulgarian legend — God, walking alone,
is followed by his own shadow; he says "Get up, comrade," and Satan emerges from the
shadow, asking that the universe be divided equally between them; God holds sway over
the living, Satan commands the dead. (2) A central Asian legend — God, alone on a rock,
wishes for a brother, spits onto the waters, a mountain rises; when God splits the
mountain with his sword the devil steps out asking to be his brother; together God and
the devil create the world.

Closing discussion: in polytheistic religions bad/mischievous spirits could be blamed
for evil, but monotheism (Judaism, Christianity, Islam) makes the problem of evil "a
thorny one: if God is all powerful and all good, how can he tolerate the existence of
evil in the world?"

## p.26-29 — Zoroastrianism, Zurvanism, Gnosticism

Some Christian creeds resolve the problem of evil by saying God created Satan "so that
the descendants of Adam would be forced to choose between the paths of righteousness and
corruption" — a position called dualism in theological scholarship.

Zoroastrianism (also called Parseeism): founded by Zoroaster (Zarathustra), thought born
near Tehran ~600 years before Christ. Miraculous birth legend: a woman named Dughdov
conceived a child whose three parts — heavenly glory, guardian spirit, bodily self —
passed through heaven into her womb; radiance so great it lit up his father's village.
Communicated with God, known in ancient Persia as Ahura Mazda, or Ohrmazd, from birth.
At about thirty, wandered the desert, had a vision, devoted his life to converting
Persians to a new faith. Revered as model for priests, warriors, farmers; also a healer,
mathematician, scholar. Earliest teachings survive as seventeen hymns called the Gathas,
focused on Ohrmazd (highest god, creator of heaven and earth) and his fierce opponent
Ahriman, spirit of evil. Ohrmazd's noble attributes personified as individuals called
Amesha Spentas ("Bounteous Immortals"); Ahriman commanded vile demons and archdemons,
the most insidious a personification of dishonesty called Lie.

Zoroastrian vocabulary reserved special words for the forces of good (head, hand,
speaking, dying) versus evil (skull, claw, howling, perishing). Devout Zoroastrians
enjoined to "drink wine and be merry." Became official faith of the Persian Empire in
the third century AD.

Zurvanism: possible offshoot sect. Taught that a higher principle, Infinite Time or
Zurvan, gave birth to twins Ohrmazd and Ahriman — opposing spirits as coequal but
subordinate products of a higher being. Ahriman was to have hegemony over the world for
9,000 years, then his brother Ohrmazd would take charge, aided by Zurvan taking his side
to vanquish his evil twin. The Good Religion (Zoroastrianism) declined after Islam's
seventh-century rise; still practiced by ~10,000 in Iran and ~100,000 Parsees in India.

Gnosticism: from Greek "gnosis," meaning "knowledge" — specifically a "knowledge of the
heart," attained through personal revelation rather than faith or liturgical
instruction. Grew as a rebellious offshoot against Judaism/Christianity. Held that
humankind possesses a spark of divine goodness (the pneuma, or "breath") trapped in an
irrevocably evil physical world. Jewish Gnostic tradition saw the Hebrew God, Yahweh, as
merely a lesser spirit, or demiurge, who created the world and death and all evil along
with it. Christian Gnostics believed Christ never truly became human (a physical body
would by nature be evil); Christ's role was to show humanity that communication with the
divine is through personal revelation. Formally suppressed as an "apostasy" by the
Catholic hierarchy in the fourth century, though it never fully died out.

## p.30-33 — Manicheanism, Bogomils, Catharism

Manicheanism: third-century, founded by Mani, born 216 CE near Seleucia-Ctesiphon
(capital of the Persian Empire). At twelve, visited by an angel called at-Taum ("twin"
in Persian), who told him to leave his father's syncretic (Gnostic/Jewish/Christian)
sect; at twenty-four, at-Taum returned and instructed him to found his own doctrine;
became Mani's spiritual "twin" companion for life. Mani proposed two fundamental
principles from the beginning: God (greatness and light) and Hylé (inherent in all
earthly matter, equal to God in power). The physical world was created from the bodies
of Hylé's followers, imprisoning sparks of divine light in plants/animals/humans to
protect them. Devotees ("the elect") practiced rigid asceticism — no material wealth, no
sexual intercourse, at most one vegetarian meal a day; lay supporters were called
"hearers." At the end of the world, the faithful would rise to "the sphere of eternal
light"; sinners and worldly baggage would be compressed into a "great round lump of
filth" called the globus and banished to darkness forever. Mani was imprisoned by
Persian ruler Bahram I, died in chains in 276 CE; his body flayed, skin stuffed with
straw and displayed. Manicheanism spread from China to Rome, survived nearly a thousand
years; Saint Augustine was a Manichean for nine years before converting to Catholicism.

Bogomils: tenth-century Bulgarian priest Bogomil taught all material things were evil,
the work of the devil (Satanael), whom he called the elder son of God and brother of
Christ. Held Christ's crucifixion was illusion; that the Virgin Mary had not truly given
birth to Christ, that he "entered her body through her right ear" and departed "again as
a phantom." Rejected baptism and the eucharist; the only sanctioned devotion was
reciting the Lord's Prayer at set times. By the fourteenth century "Bogomil" had come to
mean anyone with outlandish beliefs.

Catharism: 12th-13th century southern France/northern Italy, name from Greek "katharos"
("pure"). Cathars held Satan was the chief promulgator of evil, though not as powerful
as God. Most devout members, "the Perfect," lived barefoot, ascetic, itinerant lives,
total abstinence from meat/wine/sex/comfort. Believed Christ could not have been born of
Mary's flesh, his death on the cross and physical resurrection were fictions/illusions.
Pope Innocent III proclaimed a crusade against the Cathars in 1208. In 1209 the town of
Béziers (a Cathar stronghold) was destroyed, its 20,000 citizens put to death on order of
the Catholic bishop of Cîteaux. In 1244 the crusaders sacked the town of Montségur and
burned 200 of the Perfect, ending the last open Cathar resistance. More than a million
people were said to have been killed in the campaign.

## p.34-35 — Pythagoreans, Plato, Polarity, Yin-Yang, Shiva/Shakti

Pythagoreans (6th-5th c. BC Greek): per their intellectual descendant Aristotle, set out
ten "first principles" as a list of opposites: limited/unlimited, odd/even,
square/oblong, at-rest/in-motion, straight/crooked, one/plurality, right/left,
light/darkness, male/female, good/evil.

Plato (born Athens, 427 BC, studied under Socrates): taught that every material thing
has a corresponding, separate, eternal ideal Form; physical objects change and decay,
their ideal Form does not. Elevated intellect/reason above sensual perception.

Twentieth-century philosopher Alan Watts: the human mind tends to reduce things to
binary comparisons. Carl Jung: "the essence of the conscious mind is discrimination; it
must, if it is to be aware of things, separate the opposites."

Polarity: from Greek "polos," meaning "pivot." The yin-yang symbol: a circle half black
(yin) half white (yang), separated by a sigmoid line "curving in two directions like the
letter s," each half containing a seed/dot of the other. Yang: active, light, male,
heaven. Yin: passive, dark, female, earth. Always joined inseparably, together forming
the whole.

The I Ching, or Book of Changes: complementary opposites sprang from a single cosmic
cell called ch'i (or qi), brought into existence by the creative force called Tao.

Hinduism/Buddhism stress interconnectedness of dualities rather than separateness. Shiva
is both god of creation and destruction, identified with the male principle; his
opposite is the goddess Shakti (female principle). Saying: "Shiva without Shakti is a
corpse." Tantra teaches salvation through ritualized sexual union.

Sidebar image: Fortuna, "the four-handed Roman goddess of destiny," forces humans to
ride the wheel of fortune, "a device that allots good and bad luck by chance alone."

## p.36-38 — Astrology, Alchemy, Descartes

Astrology and alchemy as forerunners of modern science. Astrologer Dane Rudhyar's
concept of "day-force" (the personal "I") and "night-force" (the collective "we") as two
universal energies. Alchemy as "a relatively coherent philosophical attempt to reconcile
the dualities of the world and the human psyche, and in particular, the duality of
spirit and matter" — the alchemist's goal to purify/elevate base metals (and the self)
to the perfection of gold. Sidebar image: two-headed dragon, alchemical symbol of "the
dual nature of mercury — both metal and liquid"; the "hermaphrodite Rebus."

Cartesian dualism: René Descartes, born France 1596. Conceived his thesis at 23 after
three dreams on the night of November 10, 1619 (phantoms, a clap of thunder, a mysterious
book of poetry) — called the "pentecost of reason." Divided the universe into res
cogitans (mind/thought, immaterial) and res extensa (matter/body, measurable) — "I
think, therefore I am." His Dutch disciple Arnold Geulincx proposed the "two clocks"
analogy: mind and body only appear to interact, but actually run independently in
perfect pre-established harmony ("occasionalism," implied though not named here).

Sidebar image, French 17th-century engraving: "LE MIROIR DE LA VIE ET DE LA MORT" (The
Mirror of Life and Death) — a young woman, half her body a skeleton. French verse caption
(paraphrased in the English sidebar as): "To love beauty is unwise, for time destroys
it. In this world nothing lasts, everything changes, and the moment we start to live, we
start to die."

## p.40 — Hegel, Marx and Engels

Dialectical materialism: Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, building on Georg Wilhelm
Friedrich Hegel's dialectical logic (thesis/antithesis resolved through synthesis).
Marx likened human "cupidity" (greed) obstructing utopia to Zoroastrianism's Ahriman
constantly opposing Ohrmazd. Bourgeoisie vs. proletariat; abolition of private property
to dissolve the duality between individual wealth and society.

## p.41-45 — Essay: "The Dark Side of Fairy Tales"

Opening: "Once upon a time, a boy climbed a beanstalk to a giant's castle, a fairy
godmother rescued a girl from her wicked stepmother, and an ugly beast courted and won a
beautiful maiden." Discusses how fairy-tale symbolism mirrors "the fundamental dualities
of good and evil, youth and maturity, or ignorance and enlightenment."

Sidebar "Secrets from a Child's Unconscious": "Little Miss Muffet" — the spider is
"Miss Muffet's exact opposite," an example of what Carl Jung called the shadow archetype,
"an obscure and generally unexpressed side of the child's own character." "Jack and the
Beanstalk" — the hen that lays golden eggs, bags of gold and silver, and a magical harp
are "metaphors for talents and abilities that formerly lay idle and unrecognized" within
Jack.

Sidebar "Imaginary Roles for Real Parents": Cinderella — the fairy godmother and wicked
stepmother "split between them the good and bad traits of a parent." Psychoanalyst Bruno
Bettelheim on a girl who fantasized "that a cruel Martian had taken over her parent's
body." Rapunzel — heroine given at birth to an enchantress, kept in a tower from age
twelve ("significant as the approximate age of sexual maturity"); a prince frees her.

## p.46-47 — sidebar "Innocence at War with Experience"

Beauty and the Beast: Beauty's demurral to suitors read as ambivalence about sexuality.
Little Red Riding Hood: the wolf as seducer; she "unwittingly pushes the limits of
childish sexuality too far, and the wolf devours her." Beauty and the Beast: outcome more
romantic — love transforms the Beast into a handsome prince.

## p.48-49 — Chapter 2 opening: "The Battle of the Sexes"

Plato's "Symposium" (4th century BC): originally "the sexes were not two as they are
now, but originally three in number; there was man, woman, and the union of the two."
Each human was round, four hands, four feet, "one head with two faces, looking opposite
ways, set on a round neck." Zeus, aided by Apollo, "cut men in two, like a sorb apple
which is halved for pickling." "Each of us... is but the indenture of a man, and he is
always looking for his other half." "Human nature was originally one and we were a
whole, and the desire and pursuit of the whole is called love."

Genesis: God created humankind "male and female" — some Jewish/Christian commentators
read this as Adam being originally hermaphroditic, "a man on his right side and a woman
on his left," separated by God with an axe. Hindu Upanishads: Atman (Being) was lonely
until "he divided into two, from whence came Man and Woman."

## p.50-51 — Goddess-worship theories

Art historian Merlin Stone: "at the very dawn of religion, God was a woman." Paleolithic
(Old Stone Age, ~2,000,000-10,000 BC) goddess statuettes carved from coal, limestone,
mammoth-tusk ivory, some traces of red ocher ("thought to symbolize life-sustaining
blood"). Anthropologist Ruth Gruhn (McGill University): prehistoric goddess-culture
theories are "on thin ice" — only "a plausible interpretation." Neolithic (New Stone
Age, from ~10,000 BC) goddess names by culture: Egypt — Nut (swallows the sun each
evening, gives birth to dawn); Sumeria — Nammu (gave birth to both heaven and earth);
China — Nü Kwa ("she who established the patterns of existence"). Sidebar photo: modern
goddess-worshiper Donna Wilshire, cradling a statue of the Mayan goddess Ixchel.

## p.52-53 — Ggantija temple

A 25,000-year-old pocket-size goddess figure carved from mammoth tusk. Its shape "was
echoed in massive temples such as Ggantija" — built ~5,000 years ago on the Maltese
island of Gozo; inner chambers "outlined the bodies of two females," doorways symbolized
"the entrances to their wombs." Worshipers reportedly slept within the walls to
"ritually reenact death and rebirth from the body of the Mother Goddess."

## p.71-77 — Essay: "Driving the Devil Out" (the exorcism of Clara Germana Celle)

Full narrative of a 1906 exorcism case: Clara Germana Celle, a South African

and taken communion in an impure state. I must hang myself. Satan calls me!"

Over the following weeks Germana suffered violent episodes: arguing with an unseen
presence, head jerking, superhuman strength. On August 20 a nun found her thrashing,
tearing her dress, "growling and barking like a dog," breaking a bedpost. Her voice
changed mid-sentence, seemingly channeling another speaker: "Now our hour has come. Many
will be sent from hell to torture and seduce you. Woe betide you, Germana!" Father
Erasmus demanded "Who are you?" and the answer came: "I am Satan."

Afterward Germana became disruptive during services, shouting "Stop preaching, you
torture me," reportedly caused frogs to teem from the altar box, and once "rose through
the air, sailed over the worshipers, and alighted near the altar," commanding the
congregation "Pray to me." She flinched from holy medals and holy water ("burned her
skin"), spoke fluent Latin she didn't otherwise know, produced a "hellish choir" of
howling/barking/hissing/growling noises, and claimed supernatural knowledge of others'
secret sins. Father Erasmus petitioned the bishop for permission to perform a formal
exorcism.

Starting in September, the mission community gathered over four days to "reclaim

it resembled a snake," she struck a nun leaving "teeth marks around a small red wound
like a snakebite," and she levitated (once tied to a chair, "chair and all"). Her face's
veins "swollen almost to bursting." She finally issued "a harrowing, woeful moan" and her
body relaxed — the exorcism was pronounced successful. Germana said she had felt the
demon leave and was able to pray again; she "remained healed," living seven more years
before dying of tuberculosis.

## p.107-109 — The biology of altruism

Charles Darwin's 1859 "On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection" already
noted self-sacrificing behavior in some species (kin selection). Examples: zebras/moose

caller more likely to be caught). 1975: Harvard zoologist Edward O. Wilson extended
Darwin's theory with modern genetics, writing on "the morality of the gene." Minnesota
Center for Twin and Adoption Research: studies of twins reared apart found heredity
accounts for ~50% of personality-trait variation (contrary to an expected ~10%). J.
Philippe Rushton (University of Western Ontario), testing 573 twin pairs, reached
similar conclusions about heritable altruism.

Cultural case studies: the !Kung people of Botswana (formerly called "Bushmen" or
"Hottentots"), nomadic foragers who ceremonially divide food among all villagers
including those who didn't help gather it, "the worst sin among the !Kung is
stinginess." Contrasted with the Ik people of Uganda, ~1,700 miles northeast, living
behind a stockade fence, each family isolated in its own smaller stockade.

## p.110 — sidebar "A Saint Who Died of Kindness"; the Ik continued

Sidebar: Maximilian Kolbe, a Franciscan friar imprisoned at Auschwitz during WWII, in
1941 offered his own life in place of a condemned prisoner ("guards chose ten men to

## p.111 — Kitty Genovese murder; the bystander effect

March 1964, Kew Gardens, Queens, New York: Kitty Genovese was stabbed to death near her
apartment over more than half an hour while 38 witnesses saw or heard the attack and did
nothing; police weren't called until she was already dead. Psychologists John Darley and
Bibb Latané developed the "bystander effect": the more witnesses present, the less
likely any individual is to intervene, since responsibility diffuses across the group.

## p.111 — Origins of religious mysticism

Historians trace religious mysticism to shamanism. Mircea Eliade, "Shamanism: Archaic
Techniques of Ecstasy": shamanism is tied to the paradise myth — "these myths refer to a
time when communication between heaven and earth was possible... heroes and medicine men
are nevertheless able to reestablish it." Shamans enter ecstatic trance via ritual
dancing and invocation of spirits to visit heaven and hell and speak with gods, the
dead, and animals. Japanese "yamabushi" ("mountain ascetics") meditate under ice-cold
waterfalls; Hindu yogis stare at the sun; Buddhists may stare into a light source, at
circles of earth-colored clay, or at blue flowers, until trance is induced. One Buddhist
sect repeats Buddha's name 50,000 to 500,000 times daily. Sufis (Islamic mystics)
practice the "whirling of the dervishes."

## p.114-115 — Saint Teresa of Ávila

Saint Teresa of Ávila, Spanish nun born 1515, joined the Carmelite order at twenty,
founded a reformed order and supervised seventeen convents; subject to ecstatic states
during which she occasionally levitated, e.g. once lifted "about half a yard from the
ground" while approaching the altar, witnessed by Sister Anne during her canonization
investigation. Quote: "It seemed to me... as if a great force beneath my feet lifted me
up... I scarcely knew that my feet touched the ground." Critic Robert Smith's 1965 book
"Comparative Miracles" argued her levitations were unwitnessed by outsiders and occurred
in altered states of consciousness. Sidebar: an 1802 north Russian engraving of a shaman
"dancing and drumming himself into an ecstatic trance."

## p.115-117 — Saint Joseph of Copertino; stigmata; Saint Francis; Padre Pio

Saint Joseph of Copertino: witnessed levitating/flying over a hundred times, most
notably in 1645 at Assisi when he reportedly soared over onlookers including the Spanish
ambassador to the papal court, to come to rest at the foot of a statue of the Virgin
Mary. Pope Benedict XIV personally reviewed the case, citing "eye-witnesses of
unchallengeable integrity."

Stigmata: wounds resembling Christ's crucifixion wounds. First Biblical mention, Saint
Paul, Galatians 6:17. First ascetic to receive them: Saint Francis of Assisi, founder of
the Franciscan order, during a 1224 forty-day retreat on Mount Alvernia in the
Apennines — a vision of Christ on the cross left wounds on his hands, feet, and side.
Only one other man since Francis is known to have carried stigmata: Padre Pio da
Pietrelcina of Foggia, Italy — in 1918, alone in the choir on the Feast of the Stigmata
of Saint Francis, he cried out and was found "bleeding profusely from the five wounds."
Public devotion became so intense the Vatican sent an emissary to calm Foggia; the
Vatican made him wear fingerless gloves to hide the marks. Padre Pio was also reputed to
"bilocate" (appear in two places at once), reportedly seen throughout Italy and in
Austria, Uruguay, and Milwaukee, Wisconsin, while never leaving his own church.

## p.118-119 — Skeptical theories; the Virgin Mary at Lourdes and Guadalupe

Physicists Dr. Richard Mattuck (University of Copenhagen) and Dr. Evan Harris Walker
(Johns Hopkins) proposed a "thermal noise" theory of psychokinesis to explain
levitation. Author Olivier Leroy's 1928 "La Lévitation" argued levitation is a "divine
hallucination" God permits observers to perceive. Father Herbert Thurston's
early-20th-century writings suggested stigmata might arise from self-inflicted wounds
during trance, or from what he called a "crucifixion complex."

Our Lady of Lourdes: February 1858, French village, three girls gathering firewood near
Massabielle; Bernadette Soubirous, asthmatic, saw a vision of "a beautiful young girl,"
dressed in white, holding a rosary, in a grotto — the beginning of one of the most
famous Marian apparition accounts.

Sidebar "Image in a Cloak of Flowers": the Virgin of Guadalupe, 1531, atop a hill called
Tepeyacac near Mexico City — appeared to Juan Diego, an Aztec convert to Catholicism,
called herself "Santa Maria de Guadalupe," caused a garden of flowers to bloom in the
desert; when Diego unfolded his cloak before the bishop, the flowers had been replaced
by an image of the Virgin herself on the fabric — now displayed at the Basilica of Our
Lady of Guadalupe, "apparently unfaded after more than 400 years."

## p.120-121 — Lourdes conclusion; Fátima apparitions

Bernadette Soubirous returned repeatedly to Massabielle; the apparition (identifying
herself only as a lady from heaven) asked for a chapel to be built and for pilgrimage on
the 13th of each month; a spring appeared where Bernadette scratched the ground, and a
blind quarryman's sight was restored by washing with its water. Millions have since
flocked to Lourdes seeking healing.

Fátima, Portugal, WWI-era: 1916-1917, child shepherds Lucia dos Santos (9), Francisco
Marto (8), and sister Jacinta saw an angel, then, on May 13 1917, the Virgin Mary
herself — "a dazzling young woman... a white veil bordered with gold," asking the
children to return monthly. Crowds swelled to 70,000 by October 13, 1917; the
apparition reportedly caused a huge silver disk (some thought it the sun) to whirl and
throw off colored flames, "making sudden incredible movements outside all cosmic laws"
— dubbed "the dance of the sun," witnessed by the full crowd. In 1941 Lucia wrote down
the secrets entrusted to her: a vision of hell, a warning of a second world war if the
world didn't repent, and a third secret she would reveal only to Pope Pius XII.

## p.122-123 — Medjugorje apparitions; angel theology across religions

Medjugorje, Yugoslavia, beginning 1981: five/six children reported apparitions of the
Virgin Mary on a hillside; Communist authorities questioned the visionaries, arrested
priests, strip-searched nuns investigating a hoax; the apparitions reportedly continue
in a room in the parish rectory.

General angel theology: ancient Egyptians, Mesopotamians, Hittites, Persians believed in
winged guardian creatures; Egyptian "ka" (a person's otherworldly double); dualistic
Persians believed every human was attended by "heavenly twins," one good spirit and one
evil. Zoroaster declared Ahura Mazda (Ohrmazd) the one true God, recast some old gods as
"bounteous immortals" (angels), others as demons. Mani (founder of Manicheanism) was
guided by the angel at-Taum ("twin"). Taoism gives no official place to angels, but
Chinese/Japanese religion has blessing/demon-casting spirits. Buddhism's bodhisattvas
("holy Buddhas-to-be") resemble angels. Islam's angels are called "malaika," messengers
who record human deeds and report to Muhammad. Medieval angelology ranked angels with
specific functions/dominions — governing compass points, the four elements, moving
stars, tending plants, one per day/season/hour, one per newborn child.

## p.124-125 — Guardian angel anecdotes

Lydia Prince's 1975 book "Appointment in Jerusalem" describes a 1929 encounter during
Arab-Jewish conflict in Jerusalem, where a mysterious young man led her and a child
safely through gunfire and street barricades, then vanished. Author Sophy Burnham's 1990
"A Book of Angels" recounts a 1964 ski-trip near-accident in France where a stranger in
black with "luminous eyes" broke her fall then vanished without a trace. Investment
banker Jack Moorman's account of an unexplained nurse who bandaged his childhood injury
and was never seen again. Karen Hill's daughter, pulled from a culvert in the Colorado
mountains, said "there were three tiny angels helping me; they told me to hold on."

## p.126-127 — Conceptions of paradise; Emanuel Swedenborg; secular ethics

Cross-cultural paradise concepts: Celtic Avalon; Greek Elysian Fields; the Buddhist Pure
Land; the Garden of Eden; Islamic paradise as the beatific vision of the "supreme God,
the source of all good." Swedish philosopher/mystic Emanuel Swedenborg (1758) claimed to
have visited heaven directly, describing five components of the afterlife: a spirit
world, hell, and three heavens (natural, spiritual, celestial); "heaven is doing good
from good-will; hell is doing evil from ill-will." Mother Teresa, "To know God, the
ultimate source of good, is to serve him. It is Christ whom you are touching in the
poor."

Ethical philosophy: secular humanist Paul Kurtz (leader of the secular humanist
movement) argued morality doesn't require belief in divine retribution. Ethical
objectivists/absolutists (e.g. Plato) hold that good/truth/virtue are eternal,
independent principles. Immanuel Kant's "categorical imperative": act only on a maxim
you could will to become a universal law — a restatement of the golden rule. Jeremy
Bentham (1748-1832), utilitarianism: good is "the greatest happiness of the greatest
number." Subjectivism and "situation ethics" — the lifeboat dilemma as a classic
thought experiment (overloaded lifeboat, who must be sacrificed).

## p.128 — Altruism examples; cosmic duality reflection

Swedish diplomat Raoul Wallenberg, who "almost single-handedly saved thousands of
Hungarian Jews from certain death at the hands of the Nazis" in WWII; an estimated
50,000 other rescuers saved ~200,000 Jews from the Holocaust. Reflection: "It seems to
be a characteristic of cosmic duality that all things are balanced in the end. What are
two distinct and opposing entities unite to become a complete and balanced whole. But
what seems like contradiction may in fact be paradox." Psychologist/philosopher William
James: "one might say that [religion] consists of the belief that there is an unseen
order, and that our supreme good lies in harmoniously adjusting ourselves thereto."

## p.129 — Essay: "Hark! Heaven's Winged Host"

Saint Augustine (AD 354-430): "Every visible thing in this world is put under the
charge of an angel." Modern minister/author Walter Nigg: "Angels are powers which
transcend the logic of our existence." A sixth-century monk devised a heavenly hierarchy
of angel names/ranks/duties. Fourteenth-century cabalists calculated the total number of
angels at 301,655,722. A "respected nature writer" derived a formula for angel wingspan
(assuming ~154 lbs body weight) — allegedly failing to account for one "particularly
outsize member of the celestial choir, said to have stood ninety-six miles high."

## p.130-131 — sidebar "A Guide to the Celestial Hierarchy"

Sixth-century Syrian monk Dionysius wrote "The Celestial Hierarchy," dividing angels
into nine ranks/choirs in three ascending levels, embraced by Pope Gregory the Great and
Albertus Magnus; influenced Dante's "Divine Comedy," Milton's "Paradise Lost," 12th-
century mystic/abbess Hildegard von Bingen's visions, and 13th-century theologian Saint
Thomas Aquinas (dubbed "the Angelic Doctor" after his 1259 University of Paris lectures
on angels). The nine choirs, in descending rank: Seraphim (highest, "creatures of light
and fire," three pairs of flame-red wings, incite mortals to divine love); Cherubim
("fullness of knowledge," keepers of celestial records, ranks said to include Satan
before his fall from grace); Thrones ("bearers of God," pictured as a haloed head above
a fiery wheel, contemplate divine justice); Dominions (regulate other angels' duties,
hold an orb/scepter); Virtues (bestowers of grace/valor, perform divine miracles, guide
the planets); Powers (impose order on the universe, keep evil at bay); Principalities
(concern the welfare of human states/nations, guardians of religion); Archangels (concern
matters of belief, messengers of grave tidings); Angels (lowliest rank, guardians of the
human race, every physical object entrusted to their care).

## p.132-137 — sidebar "God's All-Knowing Nobles"; the Watchers legend

The four great archangels: Uriel (sent to warn Noah of the flood, "regent of the sun,"
brings the wretched souls of hell before God on Judgment Day); Raphael (cast the leader
of the Watchers into perpetual darkness, patron saint of pilgrimages); Gabriel (slew the
gargantuan offspring of the celestial rebels, "the angel of revelation," will sound the
final call at doomsday); Michael.

The Watchers legend (per the Dead Sea Scrolls): 200 angels called "the Watchers"
descended to earth to sample earthly pleasures, each took a human spouse; their unions
produced monstrous, gigantic children who devoured the world's food, then turned to
slaughtering every beast/bird/reptile/fish, and finally turned on each other in
bloodshed. God dispatched four archangels: Uriel warned Noah to build an ark; Raphael
bound the Watchers' leader and cast him into eternal darkness; Gabriel incited the
angel-children to fight one another; Michael trussed up the remaining Watchers, forced
them to witness their offsprings' deaths, and condemned them to eternal torment — only
then did the flood come to wash away the destruction.
